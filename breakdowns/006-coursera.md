# Coursera Cold Email Breakdown

**Source:** Coursera article "Cold Outreach Email Template" (Coursera Staff, updated Dec 2025)
**Template number:** 006 (one source, 3 templates analyzed)
**Subject lines:** provided for all three (quoted below)
**Performance data:** not provided. The article says all data and content in its examples were invented for illustration. It cites one outside claim, that follow-up emails are about twice as likely to get a response as the first email (attributed to a Backlinko study, not verified here), and a "30/30/50" rule of thumb with no source.

> Note on the source: this is a beginner-level education article, not a sales vendor's playbook. The three templates are illustrations and not tested campaigns. The page has no emails in images (its images are a stock photo and two logos). Everything below is paraphrase.

## The 3 templates

| # | Template | Subject line | Core idea | Words (approx) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Collaboration | "Video production partners" | A junior producer praises the person's blog and channel, says they will be in the city next week, asks for ten minutes in person | 85 |
| 2 | Sales | "Cut hiring costs by 30 percent" (with a statistic in the pre-header) | Notes a recent achievement or post, says many leaders find hiring efficiency hard, claims a cost cut, links a case study, asks for a 10 minute chat | 75 |
| 3 | Business | "Response to your need for professional copy" | Cites a shared LinkedIn connection and the prospect's public comment about finding a writer, lists packages, asks for a quick chat, attaches a brochure | 120 |

## Who it targets and why it fits

Three audiences: a content creator who could collaborate, a business leader who could buy a hiring tool, and a marketer who has publicly said they need copywriting help. The article also says the format suits job seekers, people asking for mentoring and people seeking research help, not just sellers.

## Structure

Short, plain emails with a personal opening, one line about the sender, one line of value or offer, and one small ask. Template 3 packs everything into one long paragraph, which makes it harder to scan.

## Psychology used, and why it works here

| Trigger | Where it shows up | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Liking through a specific compliment | 1 (a longtime follower of their blog and channel) | A real compliment about something specific builds rapport |
| Time-bound reason and convenience | 1 (in the city next week, can come to them) | A concrete window gives a reason to ask now, and the offer to travel lowers effort |
| Commitment minimization | All three (ten minutes, a quick chat) | A named short time is easy to accept |
| Quantified benefit | 2 (subject line and body) | A number makes the gain concrete, though an invented number backfires |
| Normalization | 2 (many leaders find this hard) | Makes the problem feel common |
| Social proof and reciprocity | 2 (a similar company's case study link) | Peer evidence plus a useful resource |
| Intent signal | 3 (their own public comment about needing a copywriter) | The strongest hook in the set, because they said it themselves |
| Borrowed trust | 3 (a shared LinkedIn connection) | A common contact adds familiarity, though a vague "mutual connection" adds little |
| Effort reduction | All three are short, plain, and written at about an eighth-grade level | Quick to read |

## Hook type and subject line pattern

- **Hooks:** audience compliment plus travel, an achievement or post (placeholder), a public statement about a need.
- **Subject patterns:** a plain topic label (low curiosity), a benefit with a number, and a "response to your need" line. The article's advice: under seven words, intriguing but clear, no spam words like "free", and a pre-header that holds an industry insight or statistic.

## CTA type

A 10 minute in-person meeting, a 10 minute chat, and a quick chat this week. All three are low-friction, and none stacks several asks. Template 3 also attaches a brochure, which the other sources in this repo advise against.

## Personalization method

A manual research step is assumed. The article's own rule of thumb is that about 30 percent of success comes from researching the audience, about 30 percent from the email's content and structure, and about 50 percent from following up (unverified).

## Follow-up and delivery guidance (paraphrased)

- Send a short follow-up about a week after the first email.
- Send on a weekday morning, since night or early-morning emails get buried.
- Track who you contacted, when, and whether they replied.
- Reply quickly when someone responds.
- Test subject lines and messages with A/B tests.

## Length, reading level, tone

About 75 to 120 words. Eighth-grade reading level, warm and friendly, a little formal in template 3 ("Dear", "Best wishes").

## Weaknesses and what could be better

- **Template 1 offers the reader nothing.** It asks to meet to discuss a "potential collaboration" without saying what the idea is or what they gain. The subject is a bare topic label with no hook, and a junior assistant's title adds no weight.
- **Template 2 relies on a placeholder and a big claim.** "Your recent achievement, post, or update" is too vague to prove research, and a headline promise of a 30 percent cost cut and a pre-header statistic are not backed by anything. Invented figures damage trust and may break advertising rules.
- **Template 3 is one dense paragraph and an attachment.** It spends most of its words on the sender's services and says it wants to "inform you" of them. The public comment is a great hook, but it gets buried. The brochure attachment adds friction and spam risk.
- **"Mutual connection" is weak when unnamed.** Naming the person or the shared group would be stronger.
- **No proof or result is given for any template,** so the structure is untested.
- **Little psychology depth overall.** It is a good beginner outline but adds few new patterns beyond what the other sources show.

## What is genuinely useful here

- The public-comment hook: answering something the prospect said publicly about a need.
- The "I will be in your city" proximity hook, with an offer to come to them.
- The pre-header advice (real insight or statistic, not a repeat of the subject).
- The reminder that follow-up carries about half the weight, and that research is a major share of success.
