# FirstSales Cold Email Breakdown

**Source:** FirstSales.io cold email template page (a cold email tool vendor)
**Template number:** 007 (one source, 8 templates analyzed, plus one worked example used to teach the structure)
**Subject lines:** provided for most (quoted below)
**Performance data:** not provided. The page says the templates are examples written for the page, not real customer emails. The customer quotes on the page praise the tool (for example, that open rates rose) and give no numbers for any template.

> Note on the source: this is a vendor page, and the templates are short illustrations. Unlike most vendor pages, its advice is restrained: it stresses true details, short length and one question. The page's images are only customer avatars. It also contains a promotional prompt for AI coding agents, which has nothing to do with writing emails and was ignored. Everything below is paraphrase.

The page also walks through a worked dental clinic example of the same shape (a new second location, a likely booking problem, a one-line offer, one question, a plain sign-off). All emails are under about 60 words.

## Who it targets and why it fits

Small sales teams, founders and local-business sellers using a cold email tool. The page has a library note covering 637 templates across 38 industries. It stresses that a template is a starting point that must be filled with true details.

## Structure (the six parts)

1. **Subject line:** short, plain, specific, like a note from a person.
2. **Opening line:** about them, with one true detail.
3. **Why them:** link the detail to a problem they likely have, in one sentence.
4. **What you do:** one line, in their words, with no feature list.
5. **The ask:** one easy question where yes, no and "not now" all move things forward.
6. **Sign-off:** just a name, with no banners or long link blocks.

## Psychology used, and why it works here

| Trigger | Where it shows up | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Effort reduction | All eight, under about 60 words | Read to the end in seconds |
| Commitment minimization | All eight (a one-word answer, a pointer, a reply either way) | The first step costs almost nothing |
| Specificity | 1, 2, 7, 8 (a real detail, a place, a post they engaged with) | Shows it was written for them |
| Easy exit | 3, 5, 6, 7 (a pointer, a check-back-later option, a promise to stop, asking if the timing is wrong) | Takes pressure off and invites an honest answer |
| Forced choice | 2 (two named times), 5 (two easy answers) | A choice between two is easier than an open question |
| Consistency | 4, 5, 6 (same-thread follow-ups that build on the first email) | Feels like one conversation |
| Self-aware honesty | 6 (assumes the timing is wrong and stops), 8 (admits it is only a guess) | Admitting a guess or a limit builds trust |
| Intent signal | 8 (they engaged with a post) | Gives the email a reason to exist |
| Social proof (optional) | 4 (one line of proof you can back up) | Only used when it is real |

## Hook type and subject line pattern

- **Hooks:** a recent detail about the company, a real detail from a local listing, an engagement signal (a LinkedIn post), a right-person question.
- **Subject patterns:** a quick question with the first name, the company plus the result, a right-person question, a same-thread "Re:" for follow-ups, a close-off line, a place name, and a post reference. The page's guidance: short, plain and specific, avoid all caps and hype, and do not sound like a newsletter.

## CTA type

Yes-or-no problem question, a 15 minute call with two named times, a pointer to the right person, a reply either way, a two-answer question (is it a priority this quarter, or later), an "is the timing wrong?" check, and an offer to share how it works. Always one ask.

## Personalization method

Fill every bracket with a true detail, and delete any line you cannot make true. The page suggests research from a company site, a local listing or a LinkedIn engagement signal. It also describes its own tool as adding AI that drafts from a company's and role's details, which is vendor marketing and not part of the template advice.

## Follow-up sequence (paraphrased)

A four-step plan: a first email with one easy question, a follow-up a few days later with a new angle, a short nudge about a week after that, and a polite last email around day 12. The page says each follow-up should add something new, follow-ups should be planned together with the first email, and the sequence should stop as soon as someone replies or opts out. It suggests testing one change at a time.

## Length, reading level, tone

About 30 to 55 words each. Very plain words, around grade 6 to 7, friendly and direct, no hype.

## Weaknesses and what could be better

- **Very short can mean thin.** At around 50 words the emails give little reason to care beyond one detail. For senior buyers, a peer proof or a sharper pain line would help.
- **Placeholder dependence.** The quality depends entirely on the true detail you fill in. A weak detail makes the email generic.
- **Engagement-signal outreach is delicate.** Saying "I saw you engaged with a post" can feel like surveillance if it is handled bluntly. Keep it light and honest.
- **No proof or results.** Nothing here shows which pattern performs best.
- **Offers are generic.** "We help [type] get [result]" needs a real, specific result to stand out.
- **Vendor framing.** The page is part of a funnel for a sending tool, so some advice (such as stopping follow-ups on reply) is partly product design, though it is still good practice.

## What is genuinely useful here

- The six-part anatomy as a quick check for any email.
- "Delete any line you cannot make true" as an anti-filler rule.
- The two-answer nudge and the polite last email, which both give an honest, easy reply.
- Offering two real times instead of an open "when are you free?".
- Planning the whole sequence with the first email and stopping it on reply.
- A plain sign-off with just a name.
