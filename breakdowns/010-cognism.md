# Cognism Cold Email Breakdown

**Source:** Cognism blog "14 Cold Email Templates to Skyrocket Your Pipeline" (published Apr 2025, updated Oct 2026), by a B2B sales data vendor
**Template number:** 010 (one source, 15 emails analyzed: 14 templates plus one assembled "build your own" example)
**Subject lines:** not provided for the 14 templates. The build-your-own section gives one sample subject ("I noticed your post about creating sales efficiencies") and subject line ideas.
**Performance data:** not provided. The article cites two outside statistics (80% of prospects prefer to be contacted by email, and 65% of new business comes from referrals), both attributed to other firms and unverified. It says the templates came from its own sales team but gives no results.

> Note on the source: these are generic fill-in-the-blank templates from a vendor, and several promote the vendor's own podcast or product. The page has no emails in images (it has a video embed and a promotional banner). Everything below is paraphrase.

## Who it targets and why it fits

B2B sellers and SDRs at companies like the vendor's own customers. The templates are mostly broad B2B outreach, with a few for specific moments: after a download, after a LinkedIn accept, after no reply, and after a referral.

## Structure

Mostly short, plain: a line about why you are writing, a value line, a proof or link, and a small ask. The build-your-own section gives a seven-step method: research, subject, first two sentences, value proposition, social proof, call to action and a P.S.

## Psychology used, and why it works here

| Trigger | Where it shows up | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Effort reduction | 14 of 15 are 120 words or less | Quick to read |
| Commitment minimization | About 13 (an interest question, a quick call, a link) | Small asks |
| Specificity | About 8 (a competitor they use, a download, a LinkedIn profile, a friend's recommendation, a post) | Shows the email is about them |
| Reciprocity | About 7 (insights, leads, a podcast, a consult, a video, a case study) | Gives value first |
| Social proof | About 4 (named customers, case studies, a customer quote) | Borrowed credibility |
| Consistency | 4 (follow-up, right person, flex and break-up emails that refer to earlier contact) | Feels like one conversation |
| Forced choice | About 4 ("this week or next", two named days with a coffee voucher) | Two options are easier than an open question |
| Quantified benefit | About 3 | Makes the gain concrete |
| Curiosity gap | About 4 | Hints at something without giving it all |
| Empathy and easy exit | 2 each (acknowledging they are busy, "maybe now isn't the right time") | Takes pressure off |
| Self-aware honesty | 2 (apologizing for reaching out, joking about the break-up) | Lowers defensiveness |
| Problem agitation | 1 (PAS) | Raises the stakes of the problem |
| Borrowed trust | 1 (the referral) | A friend's recommendation transfers credibility |
| Liking and similarity | 2 (a flattering P.S., a shared passion line) | People respond to people who feel similar |
| Authority | 1 (a named senior customer quoting a result) | A credible voice, if it is real |
| Peer norm (risky) | 1 (a made-up reply-chance figure) | Implies most peers reply, but the figure is admitted to be made up |
| Humor | 1 (the break-up email) | Memorable, but can look flippant |

## Hook type and subject line pattern

- **Hooks:** a competitor in use, a download, a LinkedIn profile or accept, a referral, a prior email, a podcast share, a post and company news (the assembled example).
- **Subject patterns (from advice, not templates):** one-word subjects, a subject that doubles as the call to action, humorous subjects, ambiguous subjects that create intrigue, and one personalized sample referencing their post. The article also says only the subject and the first five to ten words show in the inbox, so avoid "I hope you're well" and "I'm getting in touch because".

## CTA type

A quick call or chat this week or next (about half), an interest question, a link to claim a freebie, a "more insights?" question, a coffee with two windows, a demo, a request to point to the right person, a request to subscribe (a small favor), and an announced phone follow-up with a number to call.

## Personalization method

Three research avenues: business-personal (their LinkedIn bio), business-business (company news like growth or acquisitions) and personal (hobbies or recent posts). The assembled example uses the bio for the first sentence and company news for the second.

## Follow-up sequence

The templates include a follow-up, a flex follow-up with a case study, a right-person check and a break-up. The article suggests two to three emails in a cadence before the follow-up templates. It also says to test two versions of each template.

## Length, reading level, tone

Mostly 35 to 100 words, plain and friendly with a little humor. The assembled example runs about 140 words.

## Weaknesses and what could be better

- **An invented statistic as a CTA.** The build-your-own CTA states a specific chance of replying and the article admits the real rate will be much lower and that it merely sounds good. Making up a figure to pressure a reply is deceptive and should not be copied.
- **A fake P.S.** The P.S. says the sender started using the prospect's idea. If untrue, it is manipulation. Use it only if real.
- **Name-dropping with an invented quote.** The social proof sample uses a named senior customer and a quote. Use only real, permitted ones.
- **Generic templates.** Several (the introduction, the follow-up, the flex email) are plain and repeat a generic "I'd love to connect" style, with a weak "looking forward to your response" close.
- **Apologetic openers.** Apologizing for reaching out weakens the email.
- **Follow-ups just check in.** The follow-up asks if they read the earlier emails and adds a pricing link, which brings little new.
- **Mismatched advice.** The article says to avoid the word "free" but then offers a freebie with a synonym. The tip is fine, but the freebie email is still a giveaway pitch.
- **Humor and emoji.** The break-up joke may land badly with some buyers.
- **Mixed channel.** The LinkedIn template is not an email.
- **Vendor bias.** Some templates promote the vendor's own podcast.

## What is genuinely useful here

- The three research avenues, and the first-two-sentences recipe (one line about them as a person, one about their company).
- The coffee voucher with a local detail and two possible days.
- The thank-you after a download, which nurtures without pitching.
- The right-person check with an acknowledgement that they may not be thinking about this yet.
- The point that only the subject and the first few words show in the inbox.
