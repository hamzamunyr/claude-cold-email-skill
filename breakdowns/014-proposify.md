# Proposify Cold Email Breakdown

**Source:** Proposify blog "The 9 Best Cold Emails We've Seen [Examples & Templates]" by Proposify's CEO (published 2023, updated Oct 2026)
**Template number:** 014 (one source, 12 emails analyzed: 1 bad example, 10 good examples shown as images, and 1 follow-up in text)
**Subject lines:** visible in most of the example images (quoted below)
**Performance data:** not provided for any single email. The article cites claims, all unverified: Yesware tests with a 30 to 50% response rate, a DigitalMarketer test of about 12,000 emails with 125 replies (1%), a HubSpot break-up template "tested" at over 33% response, humor emails at a 46% open rate, and a freelancer who closed $400K in 30 days. It gives no method for any of them.

> Note on the source: this article is different from most in this repo, because the examples are real emails shown as screenshots, with the author's own template rewrite below each. The ten text templates are rewrites of the screenshot emails, so I counted the originals once. The page is partly a pitch for the vendor's proposal software. Everything below is paraphrase.

## Who it targets and why it fits

Mostly SaaS and service sellers writing to founders, marketers and leaders. The author's favorite example worked on him because it was short, focused on his own business and showed effort (a video of his own site). The article also covers freelancers asking for subcontracted work.

## Structure

The author's guide: pick a narrow target, find leads, make a personalized lead magnet (a short video or report about the prospect's own site), keep it short, add some humor, and track opens and clicks to time the follow-up. His example shapes include a custom video offer, a free trial with a no-risk close, a quick fix suggestion, a trigger on a careers page, a new-role congratulation and a break-up.

## Hook type and subject line pattern

- **Hooks:** a custom video of their own site, a gap on their team page with a shared interest, a new role at an account, a careers page, a competitor tool in use, a noticed site fix, a prior email, and (risky) an opened report.
- **Subject patterns:** a metaphor label, a plain "quick question", "free to chat?", a strategy line, an applicant with a hobby, a sender name with the fix, and a mix of congratulations and a follow-up reference. The author criticizes the bad email's subject ("business deal") for assuming a deal.

## CTA type

Watch a short video, try a free trial, send me your phone number (weak), reply to say whether to send a video, pick one of two times, answer which of three reasons applies, a call next week, and an offer to help with a migration.

## Personalization method

The strongest pattern is a custom lead magnet: a video or report about the prospect's own site, with their brand shown first. The author suggests recording videos only for leads who reply, and using site analytics tools for the data.

## Follow-up sequence

Several follow-up ideas: a video offer to those who reply, a break-up with three options, and a follow-up triggered by an opened report. The author suggests tracking opens and clicks to time the next touch.

## Length, reading level, tone

About 55 to 110 words, plain, informal and often humorous, reading level around grade 6 to 8.

## Weaknesses and what could be better

- **Unverified claims.** The response rates and the success stories have no method or source detail.
- **Tracking-based follow-ups.** The suggestion to follow up and mention that they opened a report can feel like surveillance and may put people off.
- **Inconsistent numbers.** The VoIP example claims one savings figure in one sentence and a different figure in another, which weakens trust.
- **Feature checklist and a phone request.** The VoIP email lists features, shows a generic opener, comes from a free webmail address and asks for their number. The author even admits calls are weaker than video or content offers.
- **Risky jokes.** The legacy platform email insults the tool they use and then hedges about whether that was appropriate. Humor can easily backfire.
- **A subject with mixed signals.** The congrats email uses a subject that looks like a thread reply to a previous call, which is confusing if there was none.
- **Hypothetical clients.** The careers-page example names real companies as clients, which only works if true and permitted.
- **Vendor framing.** Parts of the article push the vendor's software.
- **The "9 best" count.** The title says 9, but there are 10 good examples.

## What is genuinely useful here

- The custom video of their own site, with one short pain-point line. It is the strongest single idea on the page.
- The offer to record a video only for those who reply.
- The no-risk three-line close for a trial.
- The new-role email that shows you know their predecessors and asks a human question.
- The careers-page trigger with a reusable body.
- The bad-email analysis: vague, assumes a deal, and asks for a call before saying why.
