# Reachly Cold Email Breakdown

**Source:** Reachly blog "Cold email templates and format: 30 examples that book meetings in 2026", from an outbound services agency
**Template number:** 029 (one source, 32 emails analyzed: 30 templates plus a follow-up email and a LinkedIn note from its two-email sequence)
**Subject lines:** provided for every template (listed below)
**Performance data:** not provided per template. The agency says it shows a number only where a real campaign produced one. It gives overall bands (8 to 12% reply on a tight list, 10 to 20% positive reply normal) and client results (one client at an 8% positive reply rate and 4.57x return in 6 months, another with $250K in contract value, a rewrite lifting reply from 0.5-1% to 1.6%). These are the vendor's own claims and I could not verify them.

> Note on the source: an agency page whose offer is the same service pitched in most templates. Its structure is the clearest in this repo, but the examples all sell one thing. No images hold emails. Everything below is paraphrase except subject lines.

## The format it teaches

Six parts in order, under 80 words, plain text, no links: a 2 to 6 word lowercase subject, a short greeting, a signal opener (what changed at their company), a one-sentence consequence, an offer with one real number, and one yes-or-no question. The page says all 30 templates run 17 to 71 words (median 56), none carries a link, and 24 carry a number.

|---|---|---|---|

The sequence is two emails 5 to 7 days apart, with the second asking whether the topic is of interest or sits with a colleague, then a LinkedIn note, then a call and final email by day 12.

## Who it targets and why it fits

B2B sellers who run outbound at volume. Because every example sells outbound, copy the structure, not the offer.

## Hook, CTA and personalization

- **Hooks:** a role-based pattern ("[role] usually...") about 12 times, a trigger event (6), a job posting (2), a prior touch (4), an offer asset (3), a mutual connection.
- **CTAs:** a 15 minute question (about 20), an asset offer (5), a wrong-person ask (3), an interest check (3), a priority question (1), a reason-for-silence question (1).
- **Personalization:** the page's swap test says that if the detail could move to another prospect and the email read the same, it was filler. Personal details like a school or hobby are called creepy. Use AI only for the one-line signal opener.

## Sequence and cadence

Two emails, then stop. Re-engage the same list 1.5 to 2.5 months later with a new angle. A longer map goes day 1 LinkedIn visit and email, day 3 new angle, day 5 one question, day 8 a bump or easy out, day 12 call and final email, then stop. The page cites a 4 to 7 touch range from another source.

## The failures it shows

A 140 word wall of text, a "saw you went to your university and love hiking" line, and a triple ask. The fixes were cutting to about 75 words, swapping in a business signal, and asking one yes-or-no question.

## Weaknesses

- **Vendor numbers.** Client results are unverified and attached to its own service.
- **One offer repeated.** Every template pitches the same service, so the structure is stronger than the evidence.
- **Formula risk.** A funding opener only works if the prospect actually raised.
- **Rules stated as facts.** "26 of 30 start lowercase" describes its own set, not a proven winner.

## What is genuinely useful here

- The six-part format and the 80 word ceiling.
- A one-line second email that asks for either interest or a name.
- A re-engage after a couple of months with new proof or a new angle.
- The swap test for personalization.
- One yes-or-no question at the end, and no link in the first email.
- A fallback for every merge field so a blank never reads "Hi ,".
