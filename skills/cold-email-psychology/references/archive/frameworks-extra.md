# Extra Frameworks (archived from frameworks.md)

Frameworks used by only 1 or 2 analyzed emails, moved here unchanged from frameworks.md to keep the main file focused. Numbers, counts and sources are as they were. The main file lists them in its Archived frameworks index.

## 3. Idea list | Count: 2 | Source: Clay

Observation about their site > "have you considered these?" question > three ideas tailored to their business > one sentence on your tool > demo ask

- Gives free value inside the email, so the prospect learns something even if they say no.
- Best for: agencies, tools and services where you can show what you would do for them.
- Weak when: the ideas are generic or do not fit their business.

## 9. Free event invite | Count: 1 | Source: Clay

Observation > question about a trending topic > free event or session > authority proof > invite ask

## 13. Shared-problem icebreaker | Count: 1 | Source: Salesloft

Normalize the problem ("many people in your role struggle with X") > article or resource > ask what they think

- Works best when the problem is specific, not generic.

## 16. Warm visitor video | Count: 1 | Source: Salesloft

Acknowledge their interest > short intro video > teaser of what is next

- Only for people who visited or downloaded. Add one clear small ask.

## 24. 4T example (pain, effect, fix with local proof, tiny sample offer) | Count: 1 | Source: Woodpecker

Pain observation > what it does to the reader > solution with nearby proof > offer to send a small sample

- The tiny sample offer is the useful part. Skip rumor-based openers unless verifiable.

## 36. Valuable resource with inline findings | Count: 1 | Source: ZoomInfo

What you published and the data behind it > three key findings in the email > why it fits their initiative > link, no ask

- Putting the findings in the email makes it useful even if they never click.

## 39. Demo booking | Count: 1 | Source: ZoomInfo

Specific pain for their role and company size > how you fix it > ask for a short demo framed as a solution preview

- Works when the pain is real and the demo is short. A vague claim like "significantly" weakens it, so give a real number.

## 40. Match the template to the buyer stage | Count: 1 | Source: ZoomInfo

- Unaware: valuable resource or no-ask email, to educate and build trust.
- Problem-aware: PAS or recent event, to highlight the pain.
- Solution-aware: social proof or case study, to prove you can solve it.
- Evaluating options: competitor mention or AIDA, to differentiate and drive action.

## 41. Personalization tiers | Count: 1 | Source: ZoomInfo

- Tier 1: merge fields (name, company, role).
- Tier 2: a trigger-event line (funding, job change, published content).
- Tier 3: deep research (a pain point you uncovered, a mutual connection, news about their team).
- Move even part of your list to Tier 2 or 3. Match the effort to the value of the account.

## 42. Respond to a public need | Count: 1 | Source: Coursera

Reference something they said publicly about a need > who you are and one shared link > the options you offer > ask for a quick chat

- The public comment is a strong trigger. Keep it to a few short lines and do not attach a brochure.

## 43. Quantified-benefit pitch | Count: 2 | Source: Coursera, Clearout

Subject with a benefit number > pre-header with a real insight > observation about them > what you do > case study link > 10 minute ask

- Only use a number you can prove. Invented percentages in a subject line destroy trust.

## 46. Meeting ask with two real times | Count: 1 | Source: FirstSales

A reason you are writing to them and not everyone > what you do and one concrete change in the first month > 15 minutes with two named times

## 47. Two-answer nudge | Count: 1 | Source: FirstSales

Ask in two short lines whether the problem is on their list now, or whether you should return later

- Both answers are a reply, and "later" tells you when to return.

## 48. Local business listing | Count: 1 | Source: FirstSales

Name the place and one real detail from their listing or site > "we help local [type] get [result]" > ask if a short chat is worthwhile or the timing is off

## 53. Dialogue question | Count: 1 | Source: Smartlead

Reference a real post of theirs > a stage-specific observation > a genuine question about how they handle it today > a line saying you are not pitching

- A founder-to-founder tone. It starts a conversation and asks for no meeting.

## 57. Different-question follow-up | Count: 1 | Source: Smartlead

Skip making the case again > ask what would need to be true for your solution to make sense now > state that you have no agenda, offer to reconnect later

## 58. Four customization variables | Count: 1 | Source: Smartlead

Trigger event (hardest to find, highest value) > role-specific pain (not "efficiency") > proof point from the same vertical (cross-vertical proof reads as desperation) > an ask that matches their buying stage

- If you can fill all four with real detail it is a personalized email. If you only fill name and company it will perform like a template.

## 59. Direct idea pitch | Count: 1 | Source: SmartWriter

A friendly opener > "I have an idea I can explain in 10 minutes" tied to a concrete goal > one proof of the same idea working > ask for a 10 minute call

- SmartWriter says this one reportedly drew a 57% open rate and a 21% response rate (unverified). It needs a real idea and a real result.

## 61. Praise, picture, push | Count: 1 | Source: SmartWriter

Sincere praise for a real recent achievement > picture what they could do with your help while they are busy with that achievement > a small push to a short call or demo

- The praise must be real and specific. Do not pair it with an unrelated pitch.

## 63. Rock the boat | Count: 1 | Source: SmartWriter

Challenge the way they do something today > say what you do for people in their role > ask what tools they use now > ask for a quick call

- Share something they may not know about. Avoid implying their current approach is wrong without a reason.

## 66. Ready-draft pitch | Count: 1 | Source: SmartWriter

A real detail about their site or audience and a common contact if you have one > say you have a draft ready > ask one yes-or-no question about whether it fits

- Giving finished work up front is a strong form of reciprocity.

## 67. Thank-you after a download | Count: 1 | Source: Cognism

Thank them for the specific download or webinar > point to one more useful resource > ask if they would like more insights

- A nurture email, not a pitch. Keep it short and avoid listing features.

## 68. Freebie offer | Count: 2 | Source: Cognism, Reachly

Acknowledge they are busy > offer something small and useful at no cost (leads, an audit, a voucher) > a link to claim it

- Avoid the word "free" in the subject (spam filters). A synonym such as "complimentary" works.

## 71. Seven-part build with a P.S. | Count: 2 | Source: Cognism, HubSpot

Research > subject > first two sentences (one about them as a person, one about their company) > value proposition with a number > social proof with a named voice > a different kind of CTA > a P.S. that makes them feel valued

- The P.S. only works if true. A made-up claim ("we started using your idea") is deceptive.

## 72. Three research avenues | Count: 1 | Source: Cognism

Business-personal (their LinkedIn bio and focus) > business-business (company news such as growth or acquisitions) > personal (hobbies or recent posts)

## 73. Five-minute research system | Count: 1 | Source: LeadsMonky

Check their domain (rankings, ads, site) > check their top competitor > scan their LinkedIn posts > check an ad library > pick one specific thing (a keyword gap, a weak ad, a missing page) and make it your opening line

- LeadsMonky claims that referencing an activity signal lifts replies by 20 to 35% (unverified).

## 80. Busy-schedule time options | Count: 1 | Source: HubSpot

Acknowledge how busy they are > name two specific time options > add a calendar link as a backup

- Time options let them reply with a time instead of clicking a link.

## 82. Objection pre-emption | Count: 1 | Source: HubSpot

Name their likely thought ("another sales email") > say why this one differs, such as no long contract > a peer result > frame the cost as small (15 minutes) and the upside as large

- Only use a real difference. Keep the tone light.

## 83. Custom ROI calculation | Count: 1 | Source: HubSpot

Say you did quick math on their size and industry > show the assumptions and the estimated annual impact in bullets > a similar company's real result > offer the full calculation

- Show the assumptions so they can check them. Never invent the inputs.

## 84. Choose a format first | Count: 1 | Source: HubSpot

Pick the format before writing: value-led (an outcome), problem-led (a pain they feel), trigger-led (a recent event), social-proof-led (what similar companies achieved) or resource-led (an audit, benchmark or guide)

- Also use role-specific subject lines, a one-sentence bridge (a comparable company used a solution to reach an outcome in a timeframe), and one low-friction CTA.

## 86. Offer a custom video only to those who reply | Count: 1 | Source: Proposify

A follow-up that references the first email > say you made a short video with ideas for their site > ask whether to send it to them or to someone better placed to act on it

- Record the video only for people who say yes, so your effort goes to warm leads.

## 88. Personalized report lead magnet | Count: 1 | Source: Proposify

Prepare a short report or video about their own site, with their logo on the cover > send a screenshot linked to the download, not the whole report > follow up based on what they do

- Tools like a site analytics report can supply the data. Showing their own brand first proves the email is about them.

## 89. Follow-up after they open your report | Count: 1 | Source: Proposify

Refer to the report you sent > say there is room to improve > offer a short demo or a recorded webinar with a similar company result

- Risky: telling someone you saw them open it can feel like surveillance. Prefer to follow up without announcing it.

## 90. Narrow target and lead list | Count: 1 | Source: Proposify

Define the ideal customer (role, company size, revenue, industry) > build a short list of fits > research each one before writing

- Proposify's best example felt personal because it was sent to one clear fit, not a purchased list.

## 103. Seven-element annotated example | Count: 1 | Source: Saleshandy

Curiosity-based subject > a personal context opener (a real hiring or growth signal) > stage-specific pain framing > a value proposition > soft social proof (one recent team, no overclaim) > a low-friction CTA that offers a call or a brief note > a signature block with role and company

- Mirrors the best examples in this repo. Keep the proof soft and true.

## 105. Event invite, relevance first | Count: 2 | Source: Saleshandy, ProProfsDesk

Date, topic and time > tie it to something specific they have done or said > two concrete takeaways for their audience > duration and a link

- Avoid a promotional tone. Make attendance about their work.

## 106. Launch support request | Count: 2 | Source: Saleshandy, Waalaxy

Say you launched today > one line on who it helps and how > ask for an upvote or a comment > offer to return the favor

- Only for community goodwill and a real relationship. Not a cold sales pattern.

## 107. Founder-to-founder integration idea | Count: 1 | Source: Saleshandy

Say what you are building > what you noticed about their approach and the idea it sparked > where you are in your journey (exploring partnerships, pricing, integrations) > a quick call for a specific purpose

## 109. Remote and global talent outreach | Count: 2 | Source: SmartReach

Say where you are and that location does not matter > name the concrete remote benefits > ask for their preferred days, times and channel > no pressure

- A follow-up that asks about time zone and communication preferences feels considerate.

## 116. Interest-signal case study offer | Count: 1 | Source: Breakcold

Say you saw they posted an interest in a topic > one line on a result for a past client > offer to send the case study > a P.S. that shows the post you saw

- Showing the signal in a P.S. proves why you are writing.

## 117. Employee-named hook | Count: 1 | Source: Breakcold

Name a specific employee who is helping them grow > ask a question tied to what that person does > a playful P.S.

- Takes research. Keep it professional and public.

## 118. Community invite | Count: 2 | Source: Breakcold, Waalaxy

Say you are growing a community for a specific audience > its size and three topics members share > one link to join

## 120. AI-assisted personalization, done right | Count: 1 | Source: Truelist

Use AI to find signals at scale (podcast appearances, hiring spikes, launches) > a human writes the sentence about one specific thing > quote the idea, skip the compliment > offer an asset, not a meeting

- Never ship an AI first draft, and verify every reference is real. Build a library of signals first.

## 121. Multi-channel soft pivot | Count: 1 | Source: Truelist

Comment on their post a week before > then say you commented and are closing the loop by email > one insight that shifts the needle > offer a short write-up

- Only works if you really commented. Cap channels at two, and never mention a private message in a subject line.

## 122. Stage-targeted ABM | Count: 1 | Source: Truelist

Name the account trigger (a funding round, a new hire, a launch) > a specific hypothesis about the operational pain that follows that stage > a comparable company's approach > offer a one-pager that includes what you would do differently

- Volunteering a lesson learned adds credibility. Use it for your top 50 to 100 accounts, within the 60 to 180 day window after the trigger, and coordinate across your team.

## 124. Data point hook | Count: 1 | Source: Datalane

Open with one specific number about their market, say what it means for their pipeline, then ask whether the same gap shows up on their side

- The number must be real and sourced. An invented statistic here destroys the whole email.

## 126. Mentorship coffee chat | Count: 1 | Source: Salesforge

Name the exact career step you admire > say where you are now > ask one specific question about a challenge they faced > propose a 15 minute chat on their terms

- Specific research separates this from a generic request for mentorship. Offering to come to them lowers the effort.

## 127. Free pilot for case studies | Count: 1 | Source: DemandCurve

Say who you are and who you already work with > explain that you are starting out and want a few case studies > list exactly what the free work includes > say how many spots are left

- Scope it like a project brief so it does not sound too good to be true. Only claim limited spots if that is real.

## 128. Incentive follow-up | Count: 1 | Source: DemandCurve

After a silent first email, admit it is a last resort, restate the value in one line, and offer a small thank-you for a short call

- Risky: it can read as a bribe. Use it once, late in a sequence, and only if you will honor it.

## 129. Resource suggestion for their article | Count: 1 | Source: DemandCurve

Thank them for a specific guide > say you have a related piece that covers one step in more depth > hand over the link in case they want to add it

- No trade is offered, which keeps it clean. Say what is in it for their readers.

## 130. Follow-up that fixes an omission | Count: 1 | Source: Salesbread

Send a follow-up that supplies the thing you forgot, such as the link you meant to include, in one short line

- Feels like a courtesy, not a nag. Only use it if the omission is real.

## 131. CCQ personalization | Count: 1 | Source: Salesbread

Before writing, find one compliment, one commonality and one question to ask them, and build the opening from the strongest

- A research prompt, not a format. A fake commonality ruins trust.

## 132. Offer to help first | Count: 1 | Source: Outplay

Say what you noticed about their responsibilities > one line on what you did for similar customers > offer a short recording of tips > ask if that would help

- Value first, pitch later in the sequence. Only offer content you actually have.

## 133. Shared-interest opener | Count: 1 | Source: Outplay

Mention a publication or account you both follow and one thing you like about it > a soft offer to share how you helped a similar client

- The shared interest must be real and public. A forced one reads as fake.

## 134. User-feedback outreach | Count: 1 | Source: Outplay

Say you used their product or site > one real observation > offer a few improvement ideas on a call

- Only works with feedback that is true and useful to them.

## 135. Content-led connection request | Count: 1 | Source: Cleverly

Answer your buyers' top five questions as posts first > send a short connection note mentioning your niche and one high-engagement post

- Needs real posts behind it. Keep the note plain, not overly casual.

## 136. Breakdown-first ask | Count: 1 | Source: Cleverly

Offer to send a short breakdown of what worked for companies like theirs before asking for a meeting

- A smaller first yes. Follow with a case study in the next touch.

## 137. Feature, advantage, benefit | Count: 1 | Source: Artisan

Name the newest feature > turn it into what it lets them do faster > end with the business benefit and a demo ask

- Anchor it to a real prior meeting so it reads as a follow-up, not a feature list.

## 138. Beta-tester invite | Count: 1 | Source: Artisan

Say you are building something and need testers from their kind of company > state the perk > ask for a one-word reply

- Only works when the perk is real and the tester profile is specific.

## 139. Local cause favor ask | Count: 1 | Source: Artisan

Name the local cause > ask them to share a post > offer to promote them back

- A mutual-benefit ask for community brands. Do not claim urgency you do not have.

## 140. Accusatory follow-up (avoid) | Count: 1 | Source: Artisan

A follow-up that tells the prospect their own problem is their fault and calls it a last shot

- Pressure and blame lower reply rates and damage trust. Offer a reason to talk, not a diagnosis.

## 141. Free service offer | Count: 1 | Source: Mailsuite

Say exactly what is free (a review, a valuation, a check-up) > what it covers > how to claim it with a reply

- Name the scope and any limit. Avoid the word "free" in the subject line.

## 144. Donation or sponsorship ask | Count: 1 | Source: Mailsuite

Say who you are reaching and why they fit > what the money does > one ask for a short call

- Name a real outcome and amount. A generic appeal reads as a mass mail.

## 147. Soft priority check | Count: 1 | Source: Reachly

Acknowledge the inbox is busy > ask if the topic is a priority this quarter > say either answer is useful and nothing is attached

- A no-pitch bump that gets answers from people who would ignore an ask.

## 148. Reason-for-silence close | Count: 1 | Source: Reachly

Say you will stop > ask whether it was timing or fit and that one word is enough

- Gives the reader an easy reply and tells you whether to return later.

## 149. Niche segment pitch | Count: 1 | Source: Reachly

Use the segment's own words > name the ceiling of its usual growth method > describe your approach in one line > a short look ask

- Name the segment, not a generic industry. Match the tone to the audience.

## 150. Survey participation invite | Count: 1 | Source: LeadSquared

Say you are running a short survey > what it covers and how long it takes > offer early results only to participants

- Promise only what you will deliver. A reward or credit line raises participation.

## 151. Offer an introduction | Count: 1 | Source: LeadSquared

Reference their public request > offer to introduce experts who can help > ask if they want an email intro

- Pure goodwill. Only offer people who would genuinely help them.

## 152. Guilt-trip nudge (avoid) | Count: 1 | Source: LeadSquared

Tell them you miss something they stopped doing and imply they should resume

- Reads as judgment. Replace with a specific observation and a useful offer.

## 153. Insider-sourced opener (use with care) | Count: 1 | Source: LeadSquared

Cite what their staff or vendors said about a struggle and ask a question about it

- Sourcing must be real and appropriate. Unverifiable claims about their team read as surveillance.

## 154. New-leader window | Count: 2 | Source: Salesdorado, Scalelist

Mention the role they took in the last 90 days > what people in that role often cannot see yet > a similar client > ask if they are working on it

- Wait a couple of weeks after a job-change alert. Use a real quote, not an invented one.

## 155. Counterintuitive trigger line | Count: 1 | Source: Salesdorado

Name the trigger > say the first difficulty is not the obvious one but another > ask if it is an issue yet

- Only works if you really know the less obvious problem.

## 156. Honest no-reason email | Count: 1 | Source: Salesdorado

Say you have no special reason to write > share one small real field observation > ask a genuine question

- Use sparingly. A small honest sample is more credible than a big percentage.

## 157. Executive email with a public quote | Count: 1 | Source: Salesdorado

Quote something they said publicly > the peer result tied to it > offer bullets or an introduction to a peer, not a meeting

- The quote must be public, dated and accurate.

## 158. Free tool offer | Count: 1 | Source: TextMagic

Name a free tool that solves a small problem > what it outputs > a link, and an offer to talk through the result

- The tool must really be free and useful without a call.

## 160. Reply-with-a-phrase breakup | Count: 1 | Source: BluLeadz

Say you noticed no reply in 30 days > offer two exact phrases they can send back to say yes or no

- An easy out. Keep the no phrase kind and short.

## 161. Forced commonality (avoid) | Count: 1 | Source: BluLeadz

Guess a hobby or fandom and tie a pain point to it

- Reads as fake and presumptuous. Use only a shared interest they have shown publicly.

## 162. Too-good-to-be-true line (avoid) | Count: 1 | Source: BluLeadz

Opens by saying the offer sounds too good to be true

- Prompts doubt. Replace with one specific fact.

## 163. New-role priorities question | Count: 1 | Source: Waalaxy

Congratulate them on the new role > ask what priorities they are setting for their team > say you enjoy talking about it

- No pitch. A question that invites a real answer.

## 164. Reply-to-claim offer | Count: 1 | Source: Waalaxy

State the discount and the deadline > ask for a one-line reply to claim it

- Only for people who already know you. Do not use it as a cold first touch.

## 165. Cart or basket reminder (not for cold email) | Count: 1 | Source: Waalaxy

Remind them of items left behind with a link and a chat option

- This is a customer reminder, not outreach. Do not use it on cold prospects.

## 166. Clarifying second email | Count: 1 | Source: Clearout

Admit the first message may not have been clear > restate the solution in one sentence > offer a short demo

- Honest and short. Do not use it to repeat the whole first email.

## 167. Seasonal offer to a select few (avoid) | Count: 1 | Source: Clearout

Announce a time-bound discount to a few companies and ask for a chat while it lasts

- Stacks urgency and exclusivity. Only use if the deadline and the list are real.

## 168. Full-transparency pitch | Count: 1 | Source: LeadGibbon

Say this is not another sales pitch > a short demo link > who backs you and key customers > a 1-on-1 offer > ask who is the right person

- Works for hard-to-convince buyers. Name real backers and customers only.

## 169. Metric question opener | Count: 1 | Source: LeadGibbon

Ask what a specific lift would mean for their revenue or output > a short call with two times

- Use only numbers you can prove. A big unproven percentage reads as hype.

## 170. Shared-thread opener (weak) | Count: 1 | Source: Starnus

Say you both commented on the same public post and tie it to your pitch

- A shared comment thread is not a real mutual contact. Use a real referrer or skip it.

## 171. Pattern-interrupt admission | Count: 1 | Source: MailGenius

Say you were about to send the usual pitch and that it would waste their time > one real observation > an easier way > ask if they want to see the idea

- Only works if the next line is specific. Avoid gimmicks, odd formatting and cryptic subjects.

## 172. Fabricated social context (avoid) | Count: 1 | Source: Oppora

Imply that their name came up in a shared space when it did not

- Only say it if it is true and you can name the context. Otherwise use a real trigger.

## 173. Link insertion pitch | Count: 2 | Source: Influno, Semrush

Name their article and the exact section > one line on what your resource adds > the link > offer anchor text or a short summary

- Only suggest it where it genuinely helps their readers. Name the section so it does not read as a mass request.

## 174. Content-gap fix pitch | Count: 1 | Source: Influno

Praise the piece > point to one weak or missing angle > offer a resource or a sentence that fills it

- Be kind and specific. A vague criticism reads as a hook for a link.

## 176. Acquisition inquiry | Count: 1 | Source: Reachoutly

Say why their company stood out > who you are and what you acquire > ask for a brief confidential conversation, or to be pointed to the right person

- Be specific about what you look for. Vague "exploring investments" notes get ignored.

## 177. Sponsorship placement offer | Count: 1 | Source: Reachoutly

Describe your audience > why their product fits > say how many placements you are opening > offer an overview with stats and past sponsors

- Only claim limited spots if true. Give real audience numbers.

## 178. Backup-capacity pitch | Count: 1 | Source: Reachoutly

Name the route or lane > say you help when primary suppliers are tight or volumes spike > ask how they handle overflow now

- A good fit for any supplier that sells reliability when a main provider falls short.

## 179. Next-step question follow-up | Count: 1 | Source: LeadFuze

Say you are not sure what the next step is > ask what makes sense, if anything

- A one-liner that gives the reader an easy answer. Do not say they failed to reply.

## 180. Real-deadline pilot invite | Count: 1 | Source: Distribute

Say you are onboarding a small group > who it fits > the real window or batch limit > offer details before it closes

- Only use a deadline or limit that operations can verify. "Filling fast" with no proof reads as fake.

## 181. Public proof invitation | Count: 1 | Source: Distribute

Point to public examples or a documented workflow > say what is useful about it > invite them to inspect it and ask if they want the version for their stage

- Works when the proof is really public. It invites checking, not belief.

## 182. Link reclamation | Count: 1 | Source: Semrush

Thank them for mentioning you > ask for a link at the mention > supply the exact anchor text and one reason it helps their readers

- Keep it short and grateful. Making it paste-ready is the whole trick.

## 183. Broken link with a replacement | Count: 1 | Source: Semrush

Say a specific link on their page no longer works > point to your similar resource > one short line asking them to update it

- Only works if the link really is broken and your resource is a fair match.

## 184. Press pitch with a research report | Count: 1 | Source: Semrush

Praise a specific article > give one headline finding from your research > link the full report > offer quotes or more data

- Lead with the finding, not your company. Make sure it fits what they cover.

## 185. Influencer collaboration offer | Count: 1 | Source: Semrush

Say which past collaboration you liked > what you can offer (payment, product) > one question

- State the offer plainly. Match the creator to your niche.

## 186. Is-it-fair-to-say opener (risky) | Count: 1 | Source: Semrush

Ask whether it is fair to say they struggle with a specific pain, then offer a free guide

- Assumes a pain you cannot confirm. Use a real observation instead.

## 187. Last-chance note with fake urgency (avoid) | Count: 1 | Source: FreebiesMockup

A final email that says it is a last chance and that spaces are limited, with no real deadline

- Replace it with a true deadline or a plain, calm close.

## 188. Why-them, why-it-matters structure | Count: 1 | Source: Scalelist

First line: why you picked them > one line on why it matters now > one proof or idea > one easy ask

- Keep it under 80 words. The trigger does the personalizing, so the rest can stay plain.

## 189. Five-line observe-gap-offer structure | Count: 1 | Source: EmailSneak

A specific observation > the relevant gap or implication > a useful offer, not a meeting > a soft ask like "want me to send it?" > your name only

- Keeps emails at 60 to 90 words. Use the structure, write the words yourself.

## 190. Original-data offer instead of a link ask | Count: 1 | Source: EmailSneak

Praise a section of their piece > say you have new data that would update it > offer the dataset and let them decide how to use it

- Lets them choose. Only works if the data is real and relevant.

## 191. Invented-metric pitch (avoid) | Count: 1 | Source: FirstSales72

Open with a precise number about the prospect and a bracketed source such as an earnings call, a review site or social activity, then offer a peer result

- These figures are usually not available from the sources named, so they are guesses or inventions about a stranger. Use only a fact you can verify and cite.

## 192. Competitor-evaluation pitch (risky) | Count: 1 | Source: FirstSales72

Say you heard they are looking at a named competitor and that most teams comparing it also look at you

- Only say it if you know. Do not claim insider knowledge.

## 193. Bold impossible-claim opener (avoid) | Count: 1 | Source: FirstSales72

Lead with a claim that sounds fake, then ask "right?"

- Invites doubt. Replace with one provable fact.

## 194. New-fact follow-up | Count: 1 | Source: Follow-up guides

Subject: same thread. Body: one new, real development since your first email (a company event, a report, a regulatory change), one sentence on why it matters to the problem you named, then a question that can be answered in a word.

- Martal and Coldreach. If you have no new fact, wait or stop; do not invent one.

## 195. Pain-hypothesis follow-up | Count: 1 | Source: Follow-up guides

Say "I may be off, but my guess is [company] is running into [pain] as [change] happens", name the usual workaround and where it breaks, then ask "is that accurate, or not really?"

- Coldreach. Invites correction and is easy to answer.

## 196. Silence as multiple choice | Count: 1 | Source: Follow-up guides

After interest then silence, name the likely reasons (priorities moved, an internal concern, timing) and ask which one it is, and for an objection rather than a guess.

- Martal. Turns a long silence into a one-word reply.

## 197. Wrong-person routing follow-up | Count: 1 | Source: Follow-up guides

After two or three touches with no engagement, say you may have aimed this at the wrong person and ask them to point you to whoever owns the problem. If it is them, timing is useful to know too.

- Martal, Coldreach, Sendr. The cheapest ask in a sequence.

## 198. Small-win follow-up | Count: 1 | Source: Follow-up guides

Share one thing they can do now, with no strings, and offer the longer version only if they want it.

- Smartlead. Reciprocity without a meeting ask.

## 199. Post-demo team-feedback question | Count: 1 | Source: Follow-up guides
