# Email Frameworks

Emails analyzed so far: 116 (Clay, Apollo, Salesloft, Woodpecker, ZoomInfo, Coursera). Woodpecker counts are approximate.

## 1. Five-part first email | Count: 2 | Source: Clay

Context > Problem and solution > Cost of inaction > Social proof > Light CTA

- One sentence per part, under about 100 words total.
- Best for: outbound to roles where you can see a public signal (hiring, growth, a metric change).
- Weak when: you have no real signal. Generic openers read as spam.

## 2. Four-part scan structure | Count: 6 | Source: Apollo

Personalized opener > Value statement (1 to 2 sentences) > One line of proof > One question to close

- Value statement formula: "we help [who] get [outcome] without [usual pain]".
- Best for: executives who skim. Max about 3 sentences per paragraph, plain text, no attachments.

## 3. Idea list | Count: 2 | Source: Clay

Observation about their site > "have you considered these?" question > three ideas tailored to their business > one sentence on your tool > demo ask

- Gives free value inside the email, so the prospect learns something even if they say no.
- Best for: agencies, tools and services where you can show what you would do for them.
- Weak when: the ideas are generic or do not fit their business.

## 4. Event hook | Count: 2 | Source: Apollo, ZoomInfo

Recent event > the challenge it likely creates > peer result > short call ask

- Best for: funding, launches, acquisitions, big hires. Needs a real, recent event.

## 5. Problem-first | Count: 1 | Source: Apollo

Observation > peer who faced the same issue and the result > hint at a different approach > time-boxed call ask

## 6. Peer benchmark | Count: 2 | Source: Apollo, ZoomInfo

Peer result > note that the method is unusual for the industry > link to the prospect's goal > offer a short breakdown

- Best when you have a strong, shareable case study.

## 7. Industry insight | Count: 1 | Source: Apollo

Trend > what most companies do > a better way > peer result > curiosity question

- Needs real expertise and a real result, or the contrarian line sounds empty.

## 8. Referral | Count: 2 | Source: Apollo, ZoomInfo

Mutual contact > outcome achieved > fit with their goal > short call ask

## 9. Free event invite | Count: 1 | Source: Clay

Observation > question about a trending topic > free event or session > authority proof > invite ask

## 10. Quick value drop | Count: 1 | Source: Apollo

Their initiative > peer story > the insight written inline > "could this work for you?" question

- Best when you can fit a real insight in two sentences.

## 11. Resource share | Count: 2 | Source: Salesloft, ZoomInfo

Fresh resource and why you are sending it > how it connects to their pain > open offer of more help

- No hard ask, so it works as a relationship starter. Put the insight in the email or a link, not an attachment. Add one small question to invite a reply.

## 12. Case study first | Count: 3 | Source: Salesloft, ZoomInfo, Coursera

Customer result with a link > another win with the same customer > ask for a call with two named days

- Needs a real customer and real numbers. Drop hype phrases like "game changer".

## 13. Shared-problem icebreaker | Count: 1 | Source: Salesloft

Normalize the problem ("many people in your role struggle with X") > article or resource > ask what they think

- Works best when the problem is specific, not generic.

## 14. Collaboration | Count: 2 | Source: Salesloft, Coursera

Specific compliment about something they made > mutual contact > a couple of ideas > short call ask

- Needs a real piece of their content and a real mutual contact.

## 15. AIDA | Count: 3 | Source: Salesloft, Woodpecker, ZoomInfo

Attention (subject) > Interest (their post or news) > Desire (shared roadblock plus research) > Action (one CTA)

- Salesloft's version stacks three asks. Keep only one.

## 16. Warm visitor video | Count: 1 | Source: Salesloft

Acknowledge their interest > short intro video > teaser of what is next

- Only for people who visited or downloaded. Add one clear small ask.

## 17. Prior-touch follow-up | Count: 1 | Source: Salesloft

Reference the voicemail, call or message > two specific time options

- Very short. Add a reason to meet to lift replies.

## 18. Demo recap | Count: 1 | Source: Salesloft

Thanks > one-paragraph value summary > pricing and resources > open offer of more help

- A warm-only pattern. Too long and detailed for a first cold email.

## 19. Pain-point question | Count: 1 | Source: Salesloft

Personalized line > question about their current process > peers are investing in this > no-oriented question about hearing how they use it

- The personalized line carries everything. Without it the email is generic.

## 20. Five-block builder | Count: 75 | Source: Woodpecker

Subject > Personalized opener > Problem > Observation or value > Call to action

- The shared skeleton behind every Woodpecker template (69 of 75 use all five blocks). A good base to build from, but it makes emails look alike, so the opener and observation must be genuinely specific.

## 21. Follow-up reminder | Count: 27 | Source: Woodpecker, ZoomInfo

One line referencing the earlier email > restate or add one reason > one ask

- Best for: a second or third touch. Keep it under about 80 words and bring something new (a fresh reason, a short resource), not just "checking in".

## 22. PAS (problem, agitate, solution) | Count: 4 | Source: Woodpecker, ZoomInfo

Pain question > deepen the cost of the pain > present the fix with proof > ask to click or chat

- Best for: pains the prospect already feels. Avoid guarantees and insulting framing.

## 23. Before-After-Bridge | Count: 3 | Source: Woodpecker, ZoomInfo

Describe the current problem > picture the better result > bridge with how you get there > light question

- Best when you can point to a real, checkable observation (for example a metric on their site).

## 24. 4T example (pain, effect, fix with local proof, tiny sample offer) | Count: 1 | Source: Woodpecker

Pain observation > what it does to the reader > solution with nearby proof > offer to send a small sample

- The tiny sample offer is the useful part. Skip rumor-based openers unless verifiable.

## 25. Social proof pitch | Count: 5 | Source: Woodpecker

Industry or pain line > track record with named clients > "we can do the same for you" > ten minute ask

## 26. Event or office-hours invite | Count: 11 | Source: Woodpecker

Audience-targeted opener > what they will learn, when, who speaks > register or calendar link

- Gives value first. Include a recording or an alternative if they cannot attend.

## 27. Curated list with feedback ask | Count: 4 | Source: Woodpecker

Short list matched to what they said they want > "tell me which are a no, and why" > refine

- Easy to answer and gives you data. Works whenever you can prepare a shortlist.

## 28. Expired listing or stalled situation | Count: 2 | Source: Woodpecker

Empathize > give one reason it did not work > offer a fresh idea > ask to talk

## 29. Post-win thank you with referral ask | Count: 2 | Source: Woodpecker

Congratulate > offer help with leftover questions > ask them to keep you in mind for referrals

## 30. Networking introduction | Count: 7 | Source: Woodpecker

Who you are > what you noticed about them > a small coffee, chat or connect ask

- Works when the compliment is specific and the ask is easy to decline.

## 31. Candidate or employer recruiting outreach | Count: 19 | Source: Woodpecker

To candidates: profile or role trigger > why it fits > a few reasons > time ask. To employers: open role seen > proof and candidates on file > call ask.

## 32. Right-person redirect | Count: 2 | Source: Woodpecker, ZoomInfo

Say you are trying to reach whoever handles [area] > ask if that is them or someone else > one line of context with proof > thank them for the pointer

## 33. Standard intro | Count: 1 | Source: ZoomInfo

Trigger event > the challenge companies in their position usually face > what you do and for whom > "worth a conversation?"

- A three-sentence base. Needs a real trigger, or it reads as generic.

## 34. Competitor mention | Count: 1 | Source: ZoomInfo

Observed tool they use today > why others switched > a similar company's result > interest check

- Do not attack the competitor. Needs a real differentiator and a real switch story.

## 35. No-ask outreach | Count: 2 | Source: ZoomInfo

Specific compliment or observation > who you work with on the topic > offer to compare notes > state plainly that you ask for nothing

- Good for high-value accounts. Builds goodwill, but expect low replies. Follow up later with a real ask.

## 36. Valuable resource with inline findings | Count: 1 | Source: ZoomInfo

What you published and the data behind it > three key findings in the email > why it fits their initiative > link, no ask

- Putting the findings in the email makes it useful even if they never click.

## 37. LinkedIn connection follow-up | Count: 1 | Source: ZoomInfo

Thanks for connecting > one detail from their profile or company > what you do plus a proof point > 15 minute ask

## 38. Assume-not-a-priority breakup | Count: 2 | Source: ZoomInfo

Say you reached out a few times > assume it is not a priority and stop > invite a reply if that assumption is wrong

- Warns of the last message, which can lift replies. Avoid fake "close your file" gimmicks if they feel manipulative.

## 39. Demo booking | Count: 1 | Source: ZoomInfo

Specific pain for their role and company size > how you fix it > ask for a short demo framed as a solution preview

- Works when the pain is real and the demo is short. A vague claim like "significantly" weakens it, so give a real number.

## 40. Match the template to the buyer stage | Count: 1 | Source: ZoomInfo

- Unaware: valuable resource or no-ask email, to educate and build trust.
- Problem-aware: PAS or recent event, to highlight the pain.
- Solution-aware: social proof or case study, to prove you can solve it.
- Evaluating options: competitor mention or AIDA, to differentiate and drive action.

## 41. Personalization tiers | Count: 1 | Source: ZoomInfo

- Tier 1: merge fields (name, company, role).
- Tier 2: a trigger-event line (funding, job change, published content).
- Tier 3: deep research (a pain point you uncovered, a mutual connection, news about their team).
- Move even part of your list to Tier 2 or 3. Match the effort to the value of the account.

## 42. Respond to a public need | Count: 1 | Source: Coursera

Reference something they said publicly about a need > who you are and one shared link > the options you offer > ask for a quick chat

- The public comment is a strong trigger. Keep it to a few short lines and do not attach a brochure.

## 43. Quantified-benefit pitch | Count: 1 | Source: Coursera

Subject with a benefit number > pre-header with a real insight > observation about them > what you do > case study link > 10 minute ask

- Only use a number you can prove. Invented percentages in a subject line destroy trust.

## Sequences and cadence

**Four-email sequence | Count: 1 | Clay.** Email 1: full pitch. Email 2: how it works. Email 3: change the angle. Email 4: breakup, ask who else should handle it.

**Five-touch cadence | Count: 1 | Apollo.** Day 0 cold email. About day 3: share a useful resource. About a week later: a different angle. About a week later: a soft breakup. About 3 months later: re-engage with a new trigger.

**Cadence by list size | Count: 1 | Clay.**
- Large list (tens of thousands): one or two emails inside a week, then rest 45 days or more.
- Small list (a few thousand): about one useful email a month, and mix in other channels. Each email should help even if they are not ready to buy.

**Multichannel follow-up | Count: 1 | Salesloft.** If a call goes unanswered, send an email that references the voicemail. Also follow up after connecting on LinkedIn or social media.

**30/30/50 rule | Count: 1 | Coursera.** A rule of thumb that about 30 percent of success comes from researching the right audience, about 30 percent from the email's content and structure, and about 50 percent from following up. Coursera cites a Backlinko study saying follow-ups are about twice as likely to get a response as the first email (not verified here). It suggests following up about a week after the first email, and sending on weekday mornings.

**Follow-up volume and timing | Count: 1 | ZoomInfo.** Plan three to five follow-ups a few days apart before sending the first email. Each follow-up should add a new angle or value, and most replies arrive after the second or third touch. Weekday mornings (8 to 10 AM in the prospect's time zone) are suggested, which matches Apollo's advice. ZoomInfo also suggests keeping emails under about 150 words and testing subject lines and body copy as separate variables.
