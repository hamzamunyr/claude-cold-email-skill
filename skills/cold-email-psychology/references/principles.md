## How to apply each one

**Effort reduction.** Keep it short enough to read in about 20 seconds. Cut any sentence that does not earn its place.

**Specificity.** Open with something observed about the prospect: a job post, a hiring spree, a funding event, their website, a post, a podcast, a listing, a metric change. State what it likely means for them. Add a number only if it is real.

**Commitment minimization.** Make the first yes tiny. Name a short duration ("2 minutes", "10 minutes") or offer something small to receive. A small ask beats "can we book 30 minutes?"

**Curiosity gap.** Tease a result, a method or a list without giving everything. A question in the subject line works when it speaks to their real situation.

**Reciprocity.** Give something useful and free first: a short video, a breakdown, an insight, a curated list, a free session, or ideas written for their business. Then ask.

**Social proof.** Best is a company the prospect knows plus a result and timeframe. Next best is a similar company, or the fact that peers are adopting something. Never invent names or numbers.

**Consistency.** In a follow-up, refer to the earlier message in one short line, then add something new. It reads as one conversation, not a nag.

**Authority.** Mention real, verifiable groups you have worked with, original data or research, or years of experience in their market. Vague claims ("leading firm") carry little weight.

**Scarcity and urgency.** Use real deadlines only (an event date, an offer end date). Fake urgency or repeating "last chance" erodes trust.

**Unique mechanism.** Name the one thing you do that a competitor cannot say. Concrete beats generic.

**Empathy.** Name the feeling or difficulty in one line ("a tough market", "frustrating when a listing stalls") before the offer. Keep it short and sincere.

**Liking through a specific compliment.** Praise something real and specific they made or said, such as a podcast episode or a project. Generic praise reads as flattery.

**Problem agitation.** Describe the cost of the problem once, vividly, then offer relief. Do not insult them or exaggerate.

**Borrowed trust.** Use a real mutual contact who agreed to be named. Never imply a referral that did not happen.

**Loss aversion.** Ask a question that shows what they lose by waiting, such as time, money, or missed pipeline. Phrase it as a question, not an accusation.

**Easy exit.** In a final follow-up, ask who else should handle this, or give a polite way out. It takes pressure off and often routes you to the right person.

**Self-aware honesty.** A line like "you probably get many emails like this" can lower defenses. Keep it light and move on to the point.

**Contrarian angle.** Say the usual approach falls short, then hint at a different one. Only use it if you can back the claim up.

**Competitive comparison.** Show how a similar company is doing and how that compares to the prospect's goal. Executives watch peers closely.

**Similarity.** Mention a real shared role, interest or connection. Do not invent common ground.

**Quantified benefit.** Attach a real, believable number to a benefit. Do not promise a guaranteed result.

**Assumptive close (risky).** Lines that assume they will talk (a tag question like "right?" after the ask) can work with a warm lead but read as pressure in cold email. Prefer a real question.

**Forced choice.** Offer two specific options (two days, two formats) instead of an open "when are you free?".

**Normalization.** Say that many people in their role face the same problem. It makes admitting the problem feel safe. Name one real problem, not a vague one.

**Reminder of prior interest.** In a warm follow-up, mention what the person already liked or asked about so the email feels personal.

**Personal video.** A short intro video makes the email feel human. Best for warm leads who already showed interest.

**Intent signal.** When a prospect visited your site or downloaded something, say what you noticed and offer the next step. Only use this for real behavior.

**No-oriented question.** A question where "no" is the easy, safe answer can lower resistance. Keep the wording simple to avoid double negatives.

**Price transparency.** Be open about pricing when the buyer is already evaluating. It builds trust but is too much for a first cold email.

**Peer norm (risky).** Telling someone that most people like them reply can nudge a reply, but only with a real statistic. Cognism's example uses an invented reply-chance figure and admits it is for effect. Do not invent numbers.

**Humor and playfulness.** A light joke in a break-up or P.S. can make you memorable. Use it sparingly and only when it fits your brand and the prospect, since jokes can look flippant.

**Mutual benefit.** Offer a win-win, such as swapping audiences with a non-competing business, so both sides gain. Back it with a real past example.

**Exclusivity.** Signal that the opportunity is private or selective (a confidential search for a specific profile). Use it only when it is true, and do not overstate what the role or offer is.

**Convenience and accommodation.** Make saying yes easy by offering to come to them or work around their schedule ("I will be in your city next week", "any time that suits you"). Tie it to a real reason, such as a trip you are actually taking.

**No-ask framing.** Say plainly that you want nothing in return. It removes pressure and positions you as a resource. Pair it with a specific compliment or useful finding, and follow up later with a real ask.

**Topical hook.** Tie the email to a subject already on their mind, then connect it to a concrete offer. Avoid broad hype with no link to their business.
